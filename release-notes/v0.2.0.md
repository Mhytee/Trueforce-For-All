This update has been a long time coming. I have been working on it in the background ever since the plugin first launched, and it is by far the biggest release yet.

&nbsp;

Stability has come a long way, and getting there has been a community effort. Every shared log and every detailed issue report has uncovered something new about how these wheels actually talk to the PC, and "my wheel does not work" reports have dropped sharply as a result. There is more still to figure out together, and the picture of what is happening under the hood is clearer than it has ever been.

### Community features

The biggest win in this release: **once one person figures out a car's redline, fixes its name, or picks its engine layout, everyone driving the same car gets that information automatically**. The plugin's community features are now live.

- **Community preset browser** built into the Presets tab. Browse what other drivers have shared for any game or car, sorted by votes and downloads. Hover for a description, then download one to try it.
- **Share your presets and packs.** Game presets, car presets, custom engines, and multi-preset packs can all be shared. Just add a description and hit Share.
- **Car facts contribute back automatically.** When you correct a redline, fix a car's name, or pick its engine layout, the plugin offers to send that fact to the community. The next driver loading that same car gets your correction applied without ever opening the panel.
- **Stay up to date with downloaded presets.** When a curator publishes an update to a preset you have downloaded, an "updates available" chip surfaces it. Apply the update manually, or automatically.
- **Sign in with just an email.** A one-time code, no password to manage. From your account you can set a display name, see how your shared presets are doing, export your data, or delete your account.

Car-fact corrections are submitted anonymously; shared presets carry your display name. Either way, no single bad submission wins out: presets are surfaced by votes, and car facts converge as more drivers submit agreeing values.

### Car facts

For each car you drive, the plugin tracks facts about it: engine layout, redline RPM, and a car name (especially helpful when the game only gives you an ID like "Car_2267").

Getting these right matters for how the wheel feels. The Engine pulse effect models the actual firing pattern of each cylinder, so accurate cylinder count and layout are what make a V8 feel different from an inline 6 on the wheel. The rev limiter and the rev lights need the real redline RPM to fire at the right moment. Some games (Forza Horizon 4, 5, and 6 among them) do not report a redline in their telemetry at all, so the plugin has to estimate it, and that estimate is often off. A single community correction fixes it for every driver who loads that car next.

- **Variants are detected silently from telemetry.** When telemetry says "this engine is different from any I've seen for this car," the plugin creates a row in the background. No prompt, no modal. Works for Forza in-game engine swaps and on every game where telemetry exposes enough signals.

### Telemetry Based FFB

In the Forza titles, Forza Motorsport (2023) and Forza Horizon 4, 5, and 6, the plugin can now build the entire steering force itself from the telemetry the game streams, instead of passing the game's own force feedback through. It reads slip angle, tire load, and speed and synthesizes the wheel force from the ground up. The result has a real sense of the grip limit: the wheel goes light as the front washes wide, loads up through a corner, and pulls into a countersteer as the rear steps out. A per-car grip auto-calibration even learns where each car's grip tops out as you drive.

Because it replaces the wheel's force feedback wholesale, it is off by default. It works in the Forza titles today because they expose the telemetry it needs. We are exploring adding support for more games over time. It is also still being dialed in, so feedback on how it feels on your wheel is very welcome.

### Forza rev lights

With Telemetry Based FFB enabled, your wheel's rev lights now light up in Forza. They fill and flash with the engine, and honor the real redline where the community has confirmed one.

Rev lights have been surprisingly hard to deliver, for a technical reason most drivers never see: on these wheels the lights and the force feedback share a single control channel, so lighting them up while a game is driving the wheel makes that force feedback cut out. There are two ways around it. The first is Telemetry Based FFB: because the plugin is generating the force itself, the lights are free to run, which is why they work in the Forza titles now. The second is a custom driver we are building that sidesteps the conflict entirely, so rev lights could work in every game and run alongside a game's own force feedback without having to replace it. The driver has to be signed by Microsoft before it can ship, so it is still in progress.

### New effects

Three new texture effects, all off by default, built for the extra per-tire detail some telemetry exposes (the Forza titles today):

- **Axle slip.** A high scrub texture as the front washes wide, a deeper pulse as the rear steps out. Whichever axle is louder is the one losing grip, so understeer and oversteer come through as distinct feelings instead of one blur.
- **Kerb thump.** A firm whack the instant a wheel first catches a kerb, distinct from the rumble that follows it. It scales with speed, so a fast strike hits harder.
- **Lockup judder.** When a wheel locks under braking, a coarse, pulsing judder kicks in, the feel of a flat-spotted tire skidding rather than rolling, fading as the car slows. A locked wheel becomes something you feel and can correct, instead of a silent loss of grip.

### Smarter ducking

When a higher-priority effect needs to come through, ducking briefly steps back the background textures so it lands clearly. The new frequency-aware option steps back only the textures that overlap that effect in frequency, rather than all of them, so textures living in other bands keep their detail. Off by default while it is validated across wheels.

### UI/UX

The interface got a full overhaul. The old single long-scrolling panel is gone, replaced by a clean tabbed layout, and some controls now reach out of the plugin and onto SimHub's own screens.

- **A tab for each area.** Effects, Telemetry Based FFB, Presets, Controls, Settings, Account, and a Support page each get their own tab, instead of one endless panel. The Advanced settings pop-up is gone; FFB tweaks sit under a single collapsible section.
- **Presets tab.** Sortable, searchable, filterable view of every preset you own and every pack you've installed. Three segments (Games / Cars / Custom engines) plus an Installed packs view, with a persistent toggle remembering what you last looked at. Game chips filter the car list and combine with search for fine-grained narrowing. Multi-select to mass set defaults, share a pack, or batch delete.
- **Type precise values on every effect slider.** Every slider's number can be clicked and typed directly for exact tuning, not just dragged.
- **SimHub home-screen Trueforce gain widget.** The Trueforce master gain and audio capture gain live in a widget inside SimHub's home Feedback section, next to the Motors and Wind tiles, so you can adjust them without opening the plugin panel.

### Master gain control mapping

The plugin now has its own **Controls** tab for mapping master gain to a button or wheel input, so you can nudge it up and down mid-session without opening the panel.

- **Bind it on the Controls tab.** Two actions, **Master gain up** and **Master gain down**, each with an inline binder: click the field, then press the button you want. They also show up in SimHub's own Controls and events list if you would rather bind them there.
- **Adjustable step.** Set how far each press moves the gain right on the Controls tab (default 0.05).

### Rev limiter

The rev limiter no longer asks you to think in percentages of max RPM.

- **Engagement is set as an absolute RPM** instead of "% of max RPM." Existing presets that were tuned with a custom threshold are migrated to the equivalent absolute value on first use, on a per-car basis.
- **Community-shared redlines apply automatically.** When the community has confirmed a real redline for the car and engine variant you are driving, the rev limiter uses it instead of estimating. So a stock AE86 and an AE86 with a 4AGE swap each get their own community redline, picked up automatically when telemetry says you have switched to the other one. Especially valuable for games like Forza that don't expose redline in their telemetry.

### Forza Horizon

- **Every current FH6 car ships with a human-readable name.** The plugin now carries a baked-in name table for all of Forza Horizon 6's cars, so they show up as "1997 Mazda RX-7" instead of "Car_2267" out of the box.

### Bug fixes

- **Forza Horizon UDP misconfiguration no longer goes limp.** If FH was configured to send UDP telemetry to SimHub instead of directly to the plugin (Data Out pointing at SimHub's port instead of the plugin's), the plugin would take over the wheel anyway and the wheel's FFB would go limp. The plugin now detects that no Forza packets are arriving, falls back to SimHub telemetry instead of taking over, and surfaces a persistent setup banner pointing you to the right port so you can get the enhanced telemetry path working without losing FFB.

### Upgrading from older versions

When you update, your existing presets are backed up first and then migrated to the new system automatically; you should not have to touch anything. The backup lives alongside your normal settings file in SimHub's `PluginsData/Common/` folder, with a timestamped `.bak-user-preset-migration-...` filename so it stays out of the way. Keep it or delete it once you have confirmed everything still feels right.

If you had a rev-limiter engagement percentage saved on a preset, it converts to a real redline RPM value the next time you drive the car it applies to, on a per-car basis.

### Latency and responsiveness

This release sharpens the wheel's response across the board: the effects run on a faster, steadier engine, feedback holds up better when your system is under load, and audio-capture haptics reach the wheel sooner.

- **Effects now run at a steady 500 Hz.** They used to update only as fast as the game streams telemetry, often around 60 times a second, so the values effects responded to moved in coarse steps, and timing like how fast an effect builds and fades drifted with the game's telemetry rate. Now a fixed internal clock ticks 500 times a second, filling in smooth values between the game's updates, so effects follow smooth motion instead of steps, and their timing stays consistent no matter how fast a game reports.
- **Keeps feedback steady when your PC is under load.** The force-output and audio-capture threads now run at Windows' multimedia real-time priority (MMCSS "Pro Audio"), the class the system audio engine uses for itself, so a busy CPU is far less likely to interrupt them into a hitch or click.
- **Haptics generated from captured game audio reach the wheel sooner.** The capture helper now delivers audio in smaller chunks, typically cutting the audio buffer from about 8 ms to 4 ms, with no added risk of dropouts.
